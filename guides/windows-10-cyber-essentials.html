Still on Windows 10? That's a Cyber Essentials fail now — here are your real options

Windows 10 left mainstream support on 14 October 2025. Cyber Essentials requires every in-scope device to run supported software receiving security updates — so a Windows 10 machine without an Extended Security Updates arrangement doesn't get marked down, it takes the whole assessment down with it. The quiet part: most small organisations with a couple of older laptops in a cupboard have no idea this is their failure point. Ten minutes of checking fixes the surprise; here's the honest decision tree.

Reading time 4 minutesApplies to first assessments and renewals

Why “it still works fine” doesn't help you

Cyber Essentials' logic is about updates, not usability: unsupported operating systems stop receiving security patches, so every newly discovered flaw stays open forever. The requirement is that all in-scope devices run vendor-supported software — and the v3.3 rules pair this with the 14-day patching auto-fail: you can't patch within 14 days on a system that no longer receives patches at all. A single forgotten Windows 10 laptop that still touches company email or files is in scope, and it fails you.

The ten-minute fleet check

  • List every computer that touches organisational data — including home machines used for work and the “spare” under the spare desk (scope follows the data).
  • On each: Settings → System → About. Windows 11 → fine. Windows 10 → note it. Anything older → it should not be touching work data at all.
  • Check Windows 11 eligibility on the Windows 10 machines (Microsoft's PC Health Check tool): many 2018+ machines upgrade free; many older ones can't because of hardware requirements (TPM 2.0).

Your four options, honestly compared

1. Free upgrade to Windows 11 (eligible hardware)

Cost: nothing but time. If PC Health Check passes, upgrade, verify updates flow, done — the answer for most machines bought since ~2019.

2. Extended Security Updates (ESU) — the paid stay of execution

Microsoft sells ESU for Windows 10, delivering security patches beyond end-of-support (business pricing starts around £45–£50 per device for year one and doubles each year, by design). A device covered by ESU is still receiving vendor security updates, which keeps it defensible for certification during the ESU window — but declare it honestly in the assessment, keep the enrolment evidence, and treat ESU as a bridge to replacement, not a destination: the economics are built to push you off it.

3. Replace the machine

A capable business laptop costs £400–£600. Against three years of doubling ESU fees plus the risk profile of ageing kit, replacement is usually the cheaper answer by year two — and the only one that also fixes the TPM/BitLocker and performance story.

4. Take it out of scope — properly

A Windows 10 machine that genuinely never touches organisational data, on a segregated network, can sit outside scope (the workshop PC running a lathe). “We mostly don't use it” is not segregation — assessors read this one carefully, and so do we.

The renewal trap

Certified in early 2025 with Windows 10 machines? You passed under rules and support dates that no longer apply — renewal is assessed against today's standard, and unsupported OS plus the v3.3 auto-fails is the single most common way a previously certified small organisation fails its second year. Our renewal guide covers the rest of what drifts.

The order to do things

Inventory → free upgrades first → replace-or-ESU decision per remaining machine (with the doubling cost in front of you) → genuine segregation for anything staying out of scope → then certify.
