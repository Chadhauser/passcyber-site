How to choose a Cyber Essentials certification body — when they all issue the same certificate

Here's the fact the industry doesn't lead with: there are more than 350 licensed certification bodies, and the certificate is identical whichever one you use — same standard, same government backing, same listing on the public register. So you're not choosing a certificate. You're choosing service: price transparency, speed, and how you're treated when an answer isn't right. These are the seven checks that separate them.

Reading time 5 minutes

First, verify the badge — then ignore it

Any provider you consider should appear on IASME's directory of licensed certification bodies (IASME runs the scheme in partnership with the National Cyber Security Centre). Thirty seconds confirms it. After that, the badge tells you nothing more — every listed body can issue exactly the certificate a contract demands — so everything below is about how they'll treat you on the way.

The seven checks

1. Is the price published — and complete?

The honest ones publish a fixed figure and say what it includes: the assessment fee, the scheme/IASME element, VAT, and any support. The others say “contact us for a quote”, which usually means the price depends on how you sound on the phone. Insist on the all-in number in writing before you start.

2. What's the turnaround — in writing?

Basic Cyber Essentials is a verified self-assessment: once submitted, marking should take days, not weeks. Ask for the service standard in writing, and — if you're certifying for a tender deadline — say so and get the commitment against your date.

3. What happens if an answer isn't compliant?

This is the check that matters most and gets asked least. Some bodies simply fail non-compliant answers and charge for reassessment; better ones give feedback and a window to fix and resubmit. Ask exactly: how many resubmission rounds are included, within what period, at what cost? The answer predicts your whole experience.

4. Will a human help before you submit?

The questionnaire's language — scope, firewalls, user access control — trips up perfectly well-run small firms (see why applications fail). Check whether pre-submission support is included, costed, or “not our role”. A named contact beats a ticket queue.

5. Does the support cover remediation, or just paperwork?

If the readiness review finds a gap — an unsupported operating system, missing MFA — will they tell you what to fix and how, or hand you a failure and a shrug? “Done-for-you” should mean guided to a pass, not merely form-filled.

6. What do their customers say about the awkward moments?

Reviews that mention resubmissions, deadlines met, or questions answered are worth ten times the star average. You're reading for how they behave when something goes wrong — because on a first certification, something usually does.

7. Do they practise what they certify?

Ask whether the provider's own organisation holds Cyber Essentials. It's a small tell with a long shadow: a certification specialist that hasn't bothered with its own certificate is telling you what it really thinks of the standard.

Red flags — walk away

Fear-first selling (“firms like yours are being targeted — sign today”); prices that only exist verbally; “guaranteed pass” claims (the assessment is independent — a guarantee means either dishonesty or answer-fixing); and quotes that quietly bundle managed IT contracts you didn't ask for onto a certification job.

The one-page version

Confirm the IASME listing, then choose entirely on service: published all-in price, written turnaround, a real resubmission policy, human help before submission, remediation guidance included, reviews that mention the awkward moments, and a provider certified themselves. Same certificate everywhere — very different journeys.
