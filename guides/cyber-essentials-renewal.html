Why the second year catches people out

Cyber Essentials lasts twelve months. Renewal is a fresh assessment against a standard that has moved — which is why organisations that sailed through the first time sometimes fail the second.

Current question set: DanzellReading time 5 minutes

The first certification gets attention. Someone drives it, there is usually a deadline, and the organisation puts effort in. Then the certificate arrives, everyone moves on, and twelve months later it quietly expires — often noticed only when a customer asks for it.

Two separate problems live here. Remembering to renew, and passing when you do.

The standard moves

This is the one that surprises people most. Renewal is not a confirmation that nothing has changed at your end — it is an assessment against the question set in force at the time you renew, and that question set is revised between scheme years.

The current example makes the point. Organisations that certified under the previous question set could pick up a couple of major non-compliances and still pass. Under the current one, two things fail an assessment outright: multi-factor authentication missing on any cloud service that offers it, and high-risk security updates not applied within fourteen days. An organisation that scraped through on partial MFA a year ago does not scrape through now.

Check what changed before you start

Ten minutes reading what has changed since your last assessment is the highest-value part of a renewal. Going in assuming last year's answers still work is how a renewal turns into a remediation project with a deadline attached.

Your own estate moves too

Four things drift over twelve months, reliably.

Support runs out. A device or operating system that was supported at your last assessment may have reached end of vendor support since. Nothing about the machine changed — the world moved on around it, and it is now an automatic problem because unsupported software cannot receive the patches the standard requires.

New services appear. Organisations adopt tools constantly, and rarely with the assessment in mind. A new booking system, a new payroll provider, a new file-sharing tool. Each is a cloud service in scope, and each needs MFA enabled — cloud services cannot be excluded.

People change. Someone left and their account is still active. Someone joined and is using their own laptop. A volunteer or contractor has access nobody has reviewed. Access control questions are answered against the position today, not the position when you last thought about it.

Patching slips. Fourteen days is not a target you meet once; it is a state you have to be in continuously. A laptop used two days a week, or one that sat in a drawer over the summer, will not be within the window when someone finally opens it.

What expiry actually costs

More than people assume. You cannot claim current certification to customers, tenders or insurers, and you disappear from the register of currently certified organisations — which is where a diligent customer checks rather than taking your word for it.

The bundled cyber liability insurance that comes with certification for eligible organisations also ends with the certificate. That is real cover lost, not just a badge.

And recertifying after a lapse is a full assessment at the current fee. There is no cheaper route back for having held it before.

Making renewal routine

The organisations that renew smoothly do the same handful of things.

  • Diarise it at certification, not at expiry. Put a reminder in at ten months, in a shared calendar rather than one person's, with the certificate expiry date in the title.
  • Keep the inventory alive. The device, service and account list you built for the first assessment is the whole job. Update it when something changes — a new laptop, a new cloud service, someone joining or leaving — and renewal becomes a review rather than a reconstruction.
  • Enable MFA the day you adopt a service, not the month before renewal. It takes two minutes then and is an automatic failure later.
  • Check vendor support dates annually. Knowing that two machines go out of support in eight months turns a crisis into a budget line.
  • Read what has changed in the scheme before starting the assessment.
The useful way to think about it

Cyber Essentials is not an exam you pass. It describes a state your organisation is either in or not in, on the day someone checks. Organisations that treat it as an annual event find it hard every year. Organisations that keep the inventory current and switch MFA on by habit find renewal takes an afternoon.

If you are renewing now

Start from what changed: new devices, retired devices, new cloud services, people in and out, and anything that has fallen out of vendor support. Then check your answers against the current question set rather than copying last year's.
