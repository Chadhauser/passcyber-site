Why Cyber Essentials applications fail

Most organisations that fail do not fail because they are badly defended. They fail on a handful of predictable things — nearly all of which are cheaper to fix before submission than after.

For small organisationsReading time 7 minutes

Cyber Essentials is a self-assessment, marked by a qualified assessor against a published question set. That combination catches people out. Because it is self-assessed, organisations assume it is a paperwork exercise; because it is marked by an assessor, every answer has to be defensible against a specific technical requirement.

The failures cluster. Here are the ones that come up again and again, and what to do about each.

1. Software the vendor no longer supports

This is the most common single cause. Every operating system and every piece of software in scope must still be receiving security updates from its vendor. Anything past end of support is an automatic problem — not a point deduction, a fail.

The usual suspects are an old Windows machine kept because it runs one piece of equipment, an Android phone that stopped getting updates two years ago, an unsupported macOS version on somebody's older Mac, and — frequently forgotten — network hardware. A router supplied by an internet provider a decade ago is unlikely to be receiving firmware updates.

What to do

Inventory every device and every operating system version before you answer anything, and check each against its vendor's support lifecycle. Where something cannot be upgraded, the options are to replace it, or to segregate it from the rest of the network so it is genuinely out of scope — and segregation must be real, not aspirational.

2. Multi-factor authentication missing on cloud services

MFA is expected on cloud services, and especially on administrative accounts. Where a service offers it and it has not been switched on, that is a finding.

Organisations tend to enable it on the obvious things — email, banking — and miss the rest: the accounting package, the CRM, the website content management system, the file storage, the domain registrar. The domain registrar is worth singling out, because anyone who controls it controls your email.

3. The scope does not match reality

Scoping is where the most expensive mistakes happen, because a scope error discovered by the assessor means rework across every answer.

The rule that surprises people: any device used to access organisational data or services is in scope, including personally owned ones. If a director reads work email on their own phone, that phone is in scope and must meet the requirements. "We only certify the office computers" is not a scope; whole-organisation certification means the whole organisation.

Home working does not remove a device from scope either. A laptop used at home is in scope, and the requirement attaches to the device's own firewall rather than to the domestic router.

The decision to make first

Either bring personal devices up to standard and include them, or remove the need for them by providing equipment or restricting access to browser-based services with nothing held locally. Both are legitimate. What fails is leaving the question unanswered and hoping the assessor does not ask.

4. Accounts and administrative privilege

Three related findings sit here. Shared logins — one account several people use — fail, because access has to be traceable to an individual. Everyday work carried out from an account with administrative rights fails; administrators need a separate standard account for ordinary use. And accounts belonging to people who have left, or to a former supplier, must be removed rather than merely unused.

Add to that a documented process for granting and revoking access. Assessors ask how you know an account list is current, and "we would remember" is not a process.

5. Default credentials still in place

Anything with a factory-set password must have been changed — routers, wireless access points, printers, network storage, cameras, and any admin interface on a device installed by a contractor. Printers and cameras are the ones organisations forget, because nobody thinks of them as computers, and both are routinely reachable from the internet when badly configured.

6. Patching not applied promptly

Security updates rated critical or high must be applied within a short window of release, and the expectation is measured in days rather than months. Automatic updates being available is not the same as automatic updates being enabled and verified — and mobile devices, browsers and third-party applications count alongside the operating system.

A useful test

Pick any device at random and check when it last installed updates. If you cannot find out in two minutes, an assessor will not be satisfied either — and that gap in visibility is itself the problem worth fixing.

7. Answers that describe intentions

The question set asks what is in place, not what is planned. Answers phrased as "we are in the process of", "we intend to" or "generally we" are read as the control not being in place. Either the control is implemented at the point of submission or it is not; there is no partial credit, and softening the language does not help.

8. Answers inconsistent with each other

Assessors read the whole submission. A declared device count that does not match the numbers given elsewhere, a cloud service mentioned in one answer but absent from the list, mobile devices excluded in one place and referenced in another — each of these generates a query, and queries cost time. Read the completed set through as a whole before submitting, checking that every number and list agrees.

What happens if you do fail

It is not catastrophic. Certification bodies generally allow a short window in which to correct issues and resubmit without a further full fee, though the specifics vary by body and by scheme year. The real cost is time — and the awkwardness of having told a customer or funder that certification was in hand.

The pattern underneath all of it

Almost every failure above traces back to the same root: the organisation did not have an accurate picture of its own devices, accounts and services before it started answering. Build that inventory first — every device, every operating system version, every cloud service, every person with access — and most of the question set answers itself. Skip it, and you are guessing on questions where guessing is what gets found.
